## Red against yellow stone

Cherry is the red in the collection. One dress, a deep crimson rather than a
pillarbox or a wine, and the single most requested colour we run.

Nine separate sessions in the Cherry dress sit in our library. Same dress,
same roof, nine different people, and a usefully wide spread of weather. Laid
out together they answer the question people actually have, which is not "is
red nice" but "what will it look like on the day I get".

### Why it works here, in one paragraph

Everything behind you at Giza is made of the same light. Pale limestone, pale
sand, pale hazed sky, and the buff roofs of the town in between. It is a
beautiful background and a low-contrast one, and it is unforgiving of any
dress built from the same warm, light register.

Cherry is the opposite of that background in both hue and depth. It does not
need the sun at a particular angle, it does not need a clean horizon, and it
does not depend on the viewer being close enough to see detail. At any
distance, in any of the nine sessions, it reads as the subject.

### What changes is the sky, not the dress

This is the part the colour swatch cannot tell you.

On a hazy day, which is most days at Giza, the sky goes near white and the
pyramids go pale and soft. Against that, Cherry photographs a shade lighter
and slightly warmer than the fabric really is, closer to a bright scarlet. The
whole frame becomes high key: a single saturated object against a wash of pale
everything. It is the most graphic version of the photograph.

On a clear day with real blue in the sky, the same dress reads deeper and
cooler, closer to its true crimson. The pyramids pick up definition, the frame
gains a second strong colour, and the photograph becomes about the contrast
between the red and the blue rather than about the red alone.

Both are good. They are not the same picture. If you have seen a particular
red flying dress photograph you are hoping to reproduce, it is worth working
out which of those two skies it was taken under, because that is the variable,
and it is the one nobody can book.

### What the fabric does that the colour does not

Cherry is cut in a satin with a high sheen, and the sheen is doing as much
work as the hue.

Lying still across the deck, the train pools in wide soft folds and every
ridge catches a highlight, so the dress is never a flat shape. It is a surface
with light moving across it. That is why it holds up in a wide frame where the
dress is small: there is internal structure to look at.

In the air it behaves differently again. Thrown, it does not stay a sheet. It
curls, and the curl is where the colour goes darkest, because the fabric is
shading itself. The best frames in the nine sessions are nearly all the ones
where the train has turned over on itself mid throw and you can see a bright
face and a dark fold in the same piece of cloth.

The practical version of that: a thrown Cherry train is more interesting to
look at than a thrown pale one, because pale fabric in strong sun loses its
folds to highlight. Red keeps them.

### The shape of the dress itself

A deep V halter neckline, a fitted bodice to the waist, a front split, and
then an amount of train that is genuinely difficult to picture until it is on
the ground in front of you. In several of the nine frames the train fills the
entire width of the photograph, laid out across the seating deck and over the
bench behind it.

The bodice is close fitting and unremarkable, and that is the point. The dress
is adjustable and is worn by people of very different heights and builds
across those nine sessions, and the part that makes the photograph is behind
you rather than on you.

### Who it suits, and who it does not

This is the honest answer: Cherry suits the photograph more than it suits any
particular person. It is chosen by people who want the picture to be loud, and
it is the wrong choice for anyone who wants the pyramids to be the loudest
thing in the frame. In a Cherry photograph the dress wins. That is what it is
for.

If you want the plateau to carry the picture and the dress to belong to it,
the warm earth tones in the collection do that instead, and a photographer
will tell you the same thing on the roof in ten seconds.

### What it costs

A flying dress session on the rooftop is $199 per person, which includes a
dress from the collection in the colour you choose, an assistant throwing the
fabric, and the directed session. Cherry is not priced differently from any
other colour in the collection.

## FAQs

**Is Cherry red or burgundy?**
A deep crimson. Darker and cooler than a bright pillarbox red, and clearly
distinct from Hot Pink, which is the other saturated warm colour in the
collection.

**Will it look like the photographs I have seen?**
It depends on the sky more than on anything you or we control. Hazy days give
you a high key, almost graphic photograph with a near white sky. Clear days
give you a deeper red against real blue. Both are good; they are different
pictures.

**Does red work in the middle of the day?**
Yes, better than most. It holds its colour in direct sun and stays readable in
silhouette, which is not true of every colour in the collection.

**Is a red dress too much against the pyramids?**
In a Cherry photograph the dress is the subject and the plateau is the
setting. If you want it the other way round, say so and we will suggest a
colour that belongs to the background rather than fighting it.

**Does the colour cost extra?**
No. A flying dress session is $199 per person whichever of the thirteen
colours you choose.
